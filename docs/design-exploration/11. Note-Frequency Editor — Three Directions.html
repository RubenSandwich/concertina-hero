Note-Frequency Editor — Three Directions

Design pitch · Note-frequency editor

Three ways to make tuning look like your concertina

Today's editor is a plain vertical stack of rows — it works, but it doesn't read as a concertina, and it gets long fast once the instrument grows past 7 buttons. All three directions below use the same real button geometry the game draws during play (rows, split hands, the staggered anglo grid) so tuning a button happens where that button actually sits. Switch the size above — 7 is the toy's native layout, 20/30 are where a flat list stops working.

Direction A

Tap the button

The whole modal is the keyboard — the same circles, rows and bellows divider as the gameplay screen. Tap a button and a small tuner pops open right beside it.

New preset

Tap a button to tune it — type a frequency, or click 🎤 and play the note.

Why: spatial memory does the work — "the button top-left on my right hand" is faster to find here than scanning a numbered list. Trade-off: only one button is open at a time, so re-tuning every button in a row by ear means click, listen, close, click again.

Direction B

Map, then focus

A small read-only map for orientation and progress, a large detail panel for the actual editing — with Prev/Next to step through every button in physical order.

New preset

Pick a button on the map, tune it on the right — the dot turns green once it's set.

Why: big, unambiguous touch targets and a visible "how much is left" progress dot per button — this is the one that scales cleanest to 30 buttons and to phones. Trade-off: needs the width for two panes; on a narrow phone it stacks, costing a scroll.

Direction C

Shaped, all at once

Nothing hidden — every field is on screen like today — but the fields themselves sit in the instrument's real rows and stagger, hand split by a bellows divider, instead of one long list.

New preset

Tune each button to your instrument — type a frequency, or click 🎤 and play the note.

Why: no click-to-reveal step — good for comparing several buttons at once against a real instrument in your hands. Trade-off: at 30 buttons the cards get small; it leans on horizontal scroll inside the two hands rather than growing the modal.

Studio take

All three fix the actual complaint — none of them look like a spreadsheet anymore. If you only ship one: Direction B. It degrades most gracefully from 7 to 30 buttons, its detail pane is the biggest and easiest to hit on a phone, and it's the smallest lift from the current code — the existing frequency-input row becomes the detail panel almost unchanged. Direction A is the nicest fit specifically for the 7/10-button sizes if you want something more playful. Direction C is worth keeping in your pocket for a future "compare against a real instrument" power-mode, but it's the tightest fit at 30 buttons.
